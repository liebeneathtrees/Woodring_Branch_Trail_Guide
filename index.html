Getting around the map

Pan by dragging and zoom with the +/− buttons or a pinch gesture. The layer switcher in the top-right corner of the map lets you change the base map between Street map, Satellite, OpenTopoMap, and USGS Topo: the same panel also has checkboxes for Community Sightings, Trails, and Areas. When you're zoomed out, nearby pins group into a numbered circle so the map doesn't get cluttered: zoom in, or tap the circle, to spread them back apart.

Exploring species

Pins are grouped into Trees, Plants & Wildflowers, Animals, and Landmarks in the menu. Tap a pin on the map or a name in the list to open its field notes, organized into tabs: Overview, Ecology, Uses (edible/medicinal, where relevant), Caution (only shown when there's a genuine hazard), and Connections (links to ecologically related species you can tap through to). Each card also shows a Habitat badge and a Native/Introduced/Invasive status badge.

The funnel button at the top right of the map, next to the layer switcher, opens a panel with Type, Habitat, and Native status checkboxes. They work together: a pin only shows when it passes all three. The panel also shows how many pins are showing, and Show all turns every filter back on.

Trails & areas

Recorded trails appear as green dashed lines with their distance and elevation gain listed under "Trails" in the menu. Shaded areas mark the boundary of a specific place (like a meadow or a habitat zone) rather than a walking path, listed under "Areas." Both can be toggled on or off from the map's layer switcher.

Community sightings

This optional layer pulls in real, community-submitted observations from iNaturalist near the park - a separate thing from the curated species pins above, since these are individual sightings rather than field-guide writeups. Turn it on from the menu (or the map's layer switcher), then open the funnel Filters button on the map to show just birds, plants, insects, and so on. Tap any dot to see the photo, date, and a link back to the original observation.

Finding your location

The navigation arrow button near the zoom controls shows your position on the map and follows you as you walk. This needs your permission the first time, only works on the live, secure website (not a locally saved file), and works best on a phone with GPS: accuracy will vary, especially under heavy tree cover.

Saving your own pins

Long-press anywhere on the map to drop a personal pin for something you've found. First, choose whether the pin should sit exactly where you pressed, or at your live GPS location instead (useful if your press was a rough guess but your actual position is more precise). Then pick Plant, Animal, or Not Sure, and add a name or guess and any notes. These show up as solid red circles, the same size as Community Sightings dots but a different color, and are saved privately on this device.

From the "My Pins" section of the menu, you can:

Download for email: exports one pin (or all of them) as a text file to send to the park guide's maintainer for consideration, along with your own photo attached separately.

Export/Upload My Data: a different pair of buttons for your own continuity. Export saves everything to a file you keep, and Upload reads that file back in later, on this device or another one, adding anything new without creating duplicates.

This month at the park

The highlighted box near the top of the menu shows whatever's currently flowering, fruiting, or otherwise in season, based on your device's own clock: tap an entry to jump straight to that species.

Questions, comments, or suggestions

A "Questions, Comments & Suggestions" section near the bottom of the menu has a direct email link for general feedback. If you'd like to suggest a specific plant or animal be added to the guide, use a personal pin's Download option instead, so your coordinates and notes come through with it.

Woodring Branch Field Guide

An interactive record of the trees, plants, and animals found around the Woodring Branch Recreation Area at Carters Lake, near Ellijay, GA.

About & Visiting Info

The Woodring Branch Recreation Area sits on the shore of Carters Lake, a 3,200-acre U.S. Army Corps of Engineers reservoir with 62 miles of undeveloped shoreline in the Blue Ridge foothills of north Georgia. It has a day-use area with a boat ramp and picnic sites, a campground, the Oak Ridge Nature Trail, and the Amadahy Trail, a loop shared by hikers and mountain bikers. This guide is built from published sources and community observations, and it will grow over time.

Address
5026 Woodring Branch Road, Ellijay, GA 30540 - Get directions (please verify before visiting)
Getting there
From Ellijay, take Highway 282/76 west for about 11 miles to the Woodring Branch access road and follow the signs. The campground is about 4.5 miles off Highway 282, and the Amadahy trailhead is about 3.2 miles down the same road.
Phone
(706) 334-2248 (Carters Lake Resource Management Office) or (706) 276-6050 for local information
Season
A Georgia DNR map lists Woodring Branch Campground as open April 15 to October 31 (confirm current dates with the Corps of Engineers)
Fees
A day-use fee applies in the day-use area (check current rates with the Corps of Engineers). Riders report there is no fee box at the Amadahy trailhead lot.
Camping
Campers need a reservation before entering the campground (reserve on Recreation.gov), and visitors who are not registered campers are not allowed inside it. The day-use area and trailheads are separate.
Pets
Pets must be penned, caged, or on a leash no longer than six feet
Use Offline
About the Trails

Lengths and descriptions below are compiled from published sources and are approximate, and sources disagree on several numbers. Recorded trail tracks will replace them on the map as they are walked for this guide.

Amadahy Trail
About 3.5 to 4 miles, loop · Easy to moderate · Hikers and mountain bikes

The Corps of Engineers lists a 4.0-mile loop, about 1.5 hours on foot or 30 minutes by bike. Other sources give 3.5 to 3.8 miles, so treat the length as approximate. Trailhead parking is about 3.2 miles down Woodring Branch Road from Highway 282/76, and riders report a latrine at the lot. The trail descends toward the lake in either direction and climbs gradually over the last half mile back to the trailhead. Hikers report that the lake comes into view within about a mile and stays close for much of the loop, with views that are best in winter. There is a short, shallow creek crossing. Bikers should yield to walkers. Amadahy is a Cherokee word meaning forest water.

Oak Ridge Nature Trail
About 0.7 to 1 mile · Moderate

Starts in the day-use area, just south of a parking area southeast of the campground entrance. After about 700 feet it splits into two forks of roughly half a mile each. The left fork is a one-way walk along a ridge top through mixed pine and hardwood. The right fork is a loop that descends along the ridge, winds in and out of coves above the lake, and returns along a small streambed. The Corps describes a diversity of plants and animals along the way.

Carters Lake Overlook Trail
About 0.9 mile · Listed as easy

Listed on AllTrails within Woodring Branch Park. It follows an old dirt road to a bench overlooking the lake, and a right fork leads to the Oak Ridge Nature Trail. This trail is not on the Corps of Engineers trail sheet, so check the signs on site for current details.

The Amadahy Trail is the area's mountain bike trail. It is built and maintained by volunteers, so ride only on designated trails.

Native vs. Invasive at This Park

Like much of the Southeast, the land around Carters Lake carries the fingerprints of centuries of human movement. Species introduced from Asia and Europe, often planted intentionally for ornamental beauty or erosion control, have spread well beyond where they were planted, and now compete with native species across Georgia. As species get added to this guide, look for the status badge on each card to see which is which.

Even a species with some redeeming value, like feeding pollinators or birds, can still cause harm when it spreads unchecked: a landscape dominated by a few aggressive newcomers supports less overall biodiversity than one built on a full community of native species.

Native Introduced Invasive

Look for this status badge on each species card, and a small warning dot on invasive species' map pins. Where relevant, we've noted a native alternative that fills a similar role. You can help track the spread of invasive species through Georgia's Early Detection & Distribution Mapping System (EDDMapS).
